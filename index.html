02 · The hook

8:40 a.m. at Barangay San Roque Health Center

Maria Dela Cruz arrives for her prenatal check-up. The clerk types her name into the clinic's record system and gets four different files. She picks the top one. The nurse treats Maria from a record whose last visit says 2023 and which says nothing about her penicillin allergy — that note is sitting in a different file, under "Ma. Sarah Dela Cruz." Two weeks later the clinic's monthly report counts Maria three times, so the city health office is told the clinic served 212 patients when it actually served 198.

The record

Four files, three spellings, two PhilHealth numbers. Every one of them looks plausible.

The consequence

A prescription written against an incomplete allergy history. A rejected claim. A wrong number sent upward.

Who paid

Maria paid in risk. The clinic paid in rework. The city paid by buying vaccines for 14 patients who do not exist.

Nothing here is a broken computer. Every piece of software worked exactly as designed.

04 · What goes wrong

Five failure types, five downstream costs

Each failure is a category. Each category has a specific consequence at this clinic — not "data quality issues" in general.

Failure typeWhat it looks like hereWhat it causes downstream
Duplicate Two rows with the same Record ID; two more for the same woman under a different spelling. Two treatment histories. The penicillin allergy recorded in one file is invisible in the other, so a drug is prescribed that should not have been.
Conflicting Two different PhilHealth numbers for one person, both in valid format, no rule for which wins. The claim is rejected. The clinic either absorbs the cost or the patient pays cash at the counter.
Stale Last visit still reads 2023-06-15 while she has been in monthly. The prenatal risk flag never updates. She is managed as low-risk and her blood pressure trend is never seen.
Incomplete No contact number, no PhilHealth number in the newest file. The TB follow-up text never sends. She misses two appointments, and the clinic later pays for a treatment restart.
Misfiled Lourdes's record carries Maria's contact and PhilHealth number. Lourdes's consultation is charged to Maria's account — and Maria's PhilHealth record now holds another person's diagnosis. That is a privacy breach, not a typo.

Note the pattern: none of these failures stop the process. The clinic keeps working, the line keeps moving, the report still prints. Bad information fails quietly and bills you later.

05 · The cost model

"How do you measure the cost?"

Four buckets. Only two of them need an estimate; the other two you can count.

1 · Staff time

Rework, searching, re-typing, phone calls to reconcile. Here: 12 minutes per staff member per day hunting for the right Maria.

2 · Direct financial loss

Double billing, rejected claims, stock loss, write-offs. Here: rejected PhilHealth claims at ₱1,800 each.

3 · Compliance & risk

Wrong treatment, privacy breach, audit findings. Here: a prescription written against an incomplete allergy history.

4 · Trust & decision cost

Bad reports produce bad decisions. Here: a 212-patient report that hides the real 198 — and a vaccine order for 14 people who do not exist.

Worked example — one clinic, one month

4 staff × (12 min ÷ 60) × ₱120/hr × 22 days  =  ₱2,112 / month
+ 4 rejected PhilHealth claims × ₱1,800      =  ₱7,200 / month
                                                 ──────────────
                                                 ₱9,312 / month
                                                 ₱111,744 / year

for a clinic serving about 200 patients a month.

06 · Ownership

"Who is responsible for fixing the data?"

Everyone touches it, so someone has to own it. Four roles — and only one of them is accountable.

RoleWho that is at this clinicDecides / doesAccountable for
Data owner The Municipal Health Officer Decides what data is collected, who may see it, how long it is kept, and what "good enough" means. The record being fit for its purpose. Signs off. Answers to the city when it is not.
Data steward The clinic's records officer (a senior nurse) Writes the matching rule, decides which duplicate is the survivor, merges them, reviews exceptions weekly. Data quality: the merge decisions and the "search before you create" rule.
Data custodian The city IT office / the assigned encoder Runs the system: backups, accounts, access control, restores, uptime. Security and availability. Not whether the content is correct.
End user The clerk at the front desk, the midwife, the encoder Searches by ID before creating anything; flags anything that looks wrong; enters accurately. Following the rule on the desk in front of them. Nothing more, nothing less.

Fixing is shared. Accountability is named.

"Everyone is responsible for data quality" means nobody is. The clinic's fix worked because a records officer — one name — was told to merge the duplicates by a date.

The custodian is not the fall guy

"IT will fix it" is the most expensive sentence in information management. IT can restore a backup. It cannot know that "Ma. Sarah" and "Maria" are the same person. That is a stewardship decision.

✓ Exactly one row comes back. The rule on the desk says: no match means you do not create — you escalate to the records officer.

Record lookup

P-1042 opens. Last visit: 2025-02-02. One file, one tab.

✓ The number was validated against the registry during the cleanup, so there is nothing to reconcile.

The honest caveat — the fix is not free. 11 · Discussion

The two questions you should ask

Q1 · Who is responsible for fixing the data?

Everyone touches it, so someone must own it. The data owner — here, the Municipal Health Officer — is accountable for the record being fit for purpose. The data steward — the records officer — decides how duplicates get merged and writes the rule. The custodian — the city IT office — keeps it backed up and access-controlled, and is not accountable for whether the content is right. The end user — the clerk — searches before creating and flags what looks wrong. Fixing is shared; accountability is named. If nobody's name is on it, it does not get fixed.

Q2 · How do you measure the cost?

Four buckets, and only two of them are estimates. Staff time: people × minutes × hourly rate × frequency. Hard loss: rejected claims, double-billed items, stock written off — count them and multiply. Risk: price one wrong treatment or one privacy breach from what your organisation would actually pay. Trust: the decision that got made on a bad report. Add the first two for a defensible number; state the last two as risk if you cannot price them honestly. And always annualise: ₱9,312 a month sounds small; ₱111,744 a year gets a meeting.

Three prompts for the room

1
Name one record in your own enrolment or grading system you would bet is duplicated. Who would you tell, and what exactly would you want them to do?
2
The clinic can fund only one fix this year: dedupe the files, train the staff, or buy a new system. Which one, and what breaks if you pick wrong?
3
The nurse says "the duplicate is IT's fault." Using the roles table, is she right? Who is actually accountable, and for what?
12 · Takeaways

Four things to carry out of this room

1

A wrong record is not a typo — it is a second, invisible process

The clinic still ran. It just ran twice: once for the patient, once to repair the record. That second process is the cost.

2

Cost is countable, so count it

People × minutes × rate × frequency, plus one hard loss. If you cannot put a number on it, nobody will fund the fix — and always annualise.

3

Responsibility is shared; accountability is named

Owner decides, steward fixes, custodian protects, user enters. "Everyone is responsible" is how it never gets fixed.

4

Most duplicates die to two cheap things

A unique identifier, and a rule that says search before you create. Neither one is a purchase.

In-class task — due next meeting

Find one duplicate record in a system you actually use — your enrolment record, your school portal, your GCash or bank profile, your barangay's list, your family's sari-sari store notebook.

Write one page: the two records, the field that differs, who notices the problem, and what it costs in minutes or pesos. Then name the person who should be accountable for fixing it, and the rule that would have prevented it.
